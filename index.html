Can a green city really make children healthier?

A four-quadrant analysis of the spatial distribution of children and street-tree pollen potential in New York City.

Cities need trees — but a healthy green city cannot simply pursue "more trees." It must also ask which species are planted, where they are planted, and whether different communities have the capacity to respond to potential pollen impacts. This study does not oppose urban greening. It moves the conversation from planting more to planting, managing, and funding trees more scientifically and equitably.

Why children

A population with compounding exposure

Children are a priority population not simply because of "weak immunity," but because their respiratory systems are still developing — making them more susceptible to irritants like PM2.5 and pollen. Allergic symptoms and asthma affect sleep, activity, and school attendance, and children have limited ability to identify or avoid exposures on their own — leaving decisions to families, schools, and public institutions.

Not opposed to greening

What this study is

A transparent spatial-screening framework: estimate street-tree pollen-source potential, locate where it overlaps with child populations, then use four quadrants to translate the overlap into resource-allocation options.

Read carefully

What this study is not

ATPI is an indicator of potential pollen sources — not measured pollen concentration, personal exposure, or childhood allergy risk. A citywide correlation is not a diagnosis, and no tract is a "hotspot" for disease.

Core equity question — neighborhoods differ in tree species, child population, housing conditions, and public resources. Even under similar environmental conditions, families differ in their capacity to prevent, avoid, and treat allergic illness. Can policy respond to each community's actual combination of needs?

Allergenic evidence groups

172,438 trees fall into the four priority allergenic-evidence groups, out of 652,168 total living street trees — the remainder span concern, uncertain, low-concern, and unclassified categories.

Historical planting has made the London planetree a large share of NYC's street-tree basal area — Katz et al. (2024) identify Quercus, Platanus, Morus, and Betula as key contributors to airborne tree pollen citywide, forming the evidentiary basis for ATPI's species weights.

The most important social-justice finding — high-ATPI/high-child (HH) tracts clearly deserve pollen-related priority. But low-ATPI/high-child (LH) tracts hold more children overall (610,315 vs. 504,211), and the highest renter share (72.9%) and poverty rate (23.7%) of any quadrant. Allocating child-health resources by ATPI alone would overlook them. Equity means matching resources to each community's actual needs — not giving every tract the same program.

Data & Methods

Observing invisible pollen potential

Data sources

  • NYC 2015 Street Tree Census — species, location, DBH, health status for 652,168 living trees
  • Tree-pollen evidence — species classified priority / concern / uncertain / low-concern / unclassified
  • US Census Bureau ACS 2016–2020 5-year estimates by tract (B01003, B09001, S1701, B25003, B25035) — total population, child share, poverty rate, renter share, housing age. Downloaded once via the Census API and shipped as a static file; the site itself never calls the API.

Constructing ATPI

Step 1: weight by species-level allergenic evidence (priority 1.00, documented-concern 0.60, uncertain/minor 0.25, low-concern 0.05; unclassified trees excluded). Step 2: estimate basal area from DBH. Step 3: aggregate per-tree potential and divide by tract land area to get the raw density Dₜ.

Pᵢ = Wᵢ × ln(1 + BAᵢ / 0.01)
BAᵢ = π × (DBHᵢ × 0.0254 / 2)²
Dₜ = Σ Pᵢ / areaₜ

The interactive maps show a capped 0–100 relative score using the citywide 99th percentile. Statistical analysis — correlations, the four-quadrant split, the scatter plots on this site — instead uses the logged density Yₜ, since it preserves differences between tracts while reducing the influence of extreme outliers:

ATPIₜ = min(100, 100·Dₜ / Q₀.₉₉(D))
Yₜ = ln(1 + Dₜ)

Limitations

The street-tree census excludes parks, private yards, and parts of the urban forest. ATPI is a potential-source indicator, not measured pollen concentration. Child share is not childhood allergy incidence. Census-tract results cannot represent any individual child's personal exposure. Correlation does not establish causation.
